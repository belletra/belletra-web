How still is Goethe’s “rest”?

Über allen Gipfeln ist Ruh moves from silent summits and treetops to a promise: soon, you too will rest. The language is simple. The kind of rest it offers is not.

Über allen Gipfeln
Ist Ruh,
In allen Wipfeln
Spürest du
Kaum einen Hauch;
Die Vögelein schweigen im Walde.
Warte nur! Balde
Ruhest du auch.
A close, readable translationAbove every summit
there is stillness;
among all the treetops
you feel
scarcely a breath.
The little birds are silent in the forest.
Only wait—soon
you too will rest.

The meaning in one sentence

The outer landscape becomes a promise addressed to “you.”The poem begins with stillness observed above and among the trees, then turns inward: wait, and you too will join that rest. The final verb can feel consoling, ominous, or both; the text does not force one final interpretation.

Line by line

Über allen Gipfeln above all the summits
Über takes the dative here because the phrase describes location rather than motion. Gipfel can mean a summit or peak.
Ist Ruh there is rest / stillness
Ruh is the shortened poetic form of Ruhe. “Rest” preserves the later echo with ruhest; “stillness” better describes the landscape.
In allen Wipfeln among all the treetops
Wipfel narrows the view from mountain summits to the uppermost branches of trees. The echo of Gipfeln / Wipfeln binds the two heights together.
Spürest du kaum einen Hauch you feel scarcely a breath
The unusual verb-first continuation delays du. Hauch can be a breath or faint breeze: something almost absent, but still sensed.
Die Vögelein schweigen the little birds are silent
Vögelein is a diminutive of Vögel. Schweigen is not merely “make no noise”; it evokes withholding speech or song.
Warte nur! Balde ruhest du auch. only wait—soon you too will rest
Auch places the listener inside the landscape’s condition. The older balde corresponds to modern bald: soon.

Three translation decisions

Rest or stillness?“Stillness” suits the air and birds; “rest” preserves the final verbal echo and its human weight.
Above or over?Both work. “Above every summit” sounds spatial; “over all the peaks” is more compact and lyrical.
Breath or breeze?“Breeze” clarifies the weather. “Breath” lets the landscape feel more bodily and anticipates the human ending.

Why the ending remains open

Ruhen can mean to rest, lie still, or be at rest. In this sequence, the promised rest may sound like sleep after wandering, inward peace, or death. None of those possibilities can simply be substituted for the German word. The poem’s force comes from allowing natural stillness and human mortality to occupy the same language.

The Klassik Stiftung Weimar notes that Goethe wrote the verses at the Kickelhahn on 6 September 1780 and published them in 1815. Its account also calls the apparently simple poem highly artful and enigmatic—an appropriate warning against turning one suggestive reading into a final answer.

A close-reading routine you can reuse

  1. Translate the spatial frame. Mark where the scene is located before interpreting it.
  2. Track repeated sounds and roots. Here, Gipfeln / Wipfeln and Ruh / ruhest create relationships translation may lose.
  3. Watch the pronouns. The late arrival of du turns a landscape into an address.
  4. Keep ambiguous words open. Compare “rest,” “stillness,” and “sleep” without pretending they are identical.
  5. Read the ending backward. Ask how the final line changes the earlier air, birds, and forest.
  6. Return to the German aloud. Sound and pacing carry information a prose paraphrase cannot.

Frequently asked questions

What does Über allen Gipfeln ist Ruh mean?

Literally, “Above all the summits is rest/stillness.” It opens a quiet natural scene that the ending extends to the addressed listener.

Does ruhest du auch mean death?

It can support that reading, but the line does not name death. Sleep, peace, exhaustion, and mortality remain deliberately close possibilities.

Why is Ruh missing its final e?

Ruh is a poetic shortened form of Ruhe. The form helps the poem’s compact rhythm and rhymes with du.
